Rates & Banks: NL vs UK

How ECB and Bank of England rate decisions shaped what eight Dutch and British banks earn. Everything runs from 2015 to today: central bank rates update daily, bank results yearly.

Banks make most of their money from the gap between what they earn on loans and what they pay on deposits. That gap is called net interest income, and it moves with central bank rates.

When rates rise, banks raise loan rates quickly but pass on only part of the increase to savers, so the gap widens. When rates fall, especially below zero, the gap shrinks: banks rarely charge ordinary savers negative rates, so deposits become less profitable. UK banks also lock in rates for several years with a "structural hedge", which delays the effect of rate changes in both directions.

This site follows two very different rate paths. The European Central Bank kept its deposit rate below zero for about eight years and raised it late. The Bank of England stayed positive, moved first in 2021 and went higher. It tracks how eight banks fared through both: ING, ABN AMRO, Rabobank and de Volksbank in the Netherlands, and Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays and Santander UK in the United Kingdom.

Method & caveats

Banks. Netherlands: ING, ABN AMRO, Rabobank, de Volksbank (now ASN Bank; called SNS Bank before 2017). United Kingdom: Lloyds, NatWest (called RBS until 2020), Barclays, Santander UK. HSBC is left out because most of its profit comes from Asia.

Rates. The ECB deposit facility rate is the rate that matters most for euro-area banks with excess reserves. Bank Rate is the Bank of England's policy rate. Both are downloaded every morning from the central banks' own data feeds. Dates are effective dates.

Years covered. Everything starts in 2015: central bank rates from January 2015, bank results from the 2015 financial year, the earliest year with reasonably comparable public reporting for all eight banks. Some early years are missing for Rabobank (2017–2020 income and margin), ABN AMRO (2015 and most of 2016), Barclays (2015 income and return) and Lloyds (2015 return).

Net interest income. As reported each year. ING includes the TLTRO effect in 2021–22 and excludes it in 2020. Lloyds uses its underlying figure because insurance accounting distorts the statutory one. Rabobank 2022 comes from the EBA stress test. Barclays 2016–17 is restated for the sale of its African business. De Volksbank changed how it presents hedge results in 2025, which adds roughly €110m.

Returns. Dutch banks report return on equity, UK banks return on tangible equity; compare trends more than levels. ING's 2015–2018 figures are "underlying" (excluding one-offs), as it reported then. Santander UK's 2019–2022 figures are adjusted. Lloyds changed its RoTE basis in 2019 and restated 2022 for IFRS 17.

Margins. Banks change their margin definitions: NatWest changed its basis in 2021 and again in 2024, and ING's 2015–2018 figures cover the bank only. A sudden jump in one year can be a reporting change rather than a real change.

Profit. Each bank's headline figure, so definitions differ (see the Profit basis column). Use it to compare years within one bank.

Index. The income chart sets each bank's first year in the chosen range to 100. A bank missing that year starts from its first available year.

Causes. The charts show timing and size, not proof of cause. Acquisitions, loan losses, bank taxes, fines and one-off items (such as ING's 2018 settlement and 2022 Russia provisions, or RBS's legacy conduct costs) also move the numbers.
